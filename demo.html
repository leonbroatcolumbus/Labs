Agent in a Day

Watch it work on its own

Two demonstrations, then a conversation about your own idea.

35 minutes · No keyboards for this one · Follows Lab 3

Nothing to build here
Both of these are run from the front. Watch, then we talk. The second half is a discussion at your table about the idea you nominated.

Satnav, or self-driving

Everything you have built today is a satnav. It knows things you do not, and it does nothing until you ask it a question.

A trigger is the difference between that and a car that pulls away on its own, because the time has come to leave. Nobody asked it to.

Both are useful. They need very different amounts of trust.

What you are about to watch

A fault report arrives by email. Nobody opens a chat. Nobody asks the agent anything.

  1. The email lands in a monitored mailbox. It does not say P-207 — it says “the feed pump in Pump House 2, bay B”, and mentions it has been noisier than usual for a couple of days.
  2. The agent reads it, works out which asset that is, and looks it up in the manuals.
  3. A maintenance request appears in the planner’s list — drafted before anyone had read the email.
  4. Then we open the activity log and read what it actually did, in order.
Autonomy is delegated. Accountability is not.
The agent did the work. Somebody still owns the outcome. The log is what makes that acceptable rather than unnerving — you can see what it did, in what order, and why.

One thing about cost

An agent that runs on its own runs whether or not anyone needed it to, and every run costs something. The agent that becomes popular is the one most likely to become expensive — and the one most likely to stop working when it runs out.

That is not a reason to avoid this. It is a reason to ask for a limit on it.

Turn the dial, don’t flip the switch

Most real use cases sit at two or three, not five. The dial is a design choice, not a maturity model — nobody is failing by staying at two.

The agent you built this morning is at two. It drafts the request and waits to be told.

Who could say no?

Now think about your own idea rather than the pump. If you wanted to build it for real on Monday, who would have to agree?

WhoWhat they actually want

Legal or complianceA record of what happened and why
Whoever paysA number, and to know what happens if it gets popular
People whose work changesTo know whether this replaces them
A sponsorSomething visible they can point at
Two things worth knowing
Most of these people want what you want: something that works and does not blow up. And most opposition is about not being told, rather than about the technology.

For your idea — what could go wrong, and what data does it touch?

Who would need to approve it? Name people, not departments.

Who could sponsor it?
